# Does being named by AI prove product quality?

Wrong About You classified 113 wrong, 5 stale and 880 unverifiable statements among 2,922 statements about 70 vendors, from answers captured 22 and 23 September 2026. These were assessments of answer claims, not product tests. Being named by AI therefore does not prove product quality or replace checking whether a product fits your need.

## What we measured

**Measured.** The [Wrong About You method](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23270124) extracted product statements from captured answers and compared them with vendors' own public pages read 8 October 2026. It labelled 1,924 true, 113 wrong, 5 stale and 880 unverifiable. That left 2,042 checkable statements and 118 wrong-or-stale labels. Team review remains in progress.

The labels concern statements against dated evidence. A true label is not a hands-on product test, and an unverifiable label is not automatically a false statement. The study did not measure implementation quality, reliability in use or buyer satisfaction.

These observations cannot rank the quality of the 70 vendors or predict a future answer.

## What this means for you

**Reasoned.** Ask two questions separately: did the answer name the product, and is its description supported? Then evaluate the product against the requirements you actually have. A clear source passage may establish a stated feature without establishing how well it works in your situation.

If an answer names a product because of an incorrect feature description, keep both observations. Removing the error does not prove that the product is poor; leaving the name in a shortlist does not make the error harmless either.

- **If you buy software:** verify the important claims on dated primary pages and seek evidence appropriate to performance or reliability requirements.
- **If you sell software:** correct unsupported descriptions with a source, without presenting the correction as a request to certify product quality.
- **If you publish a comparison:** distinguish a vendor's stated feature from tested functionality and unknown evidence.

Frequent appearances may justify investigating a product, but they do not settle that evaluation. The useful next step follows the buyer's requirement and the available evidence, rather than the frequency of a name alone.
