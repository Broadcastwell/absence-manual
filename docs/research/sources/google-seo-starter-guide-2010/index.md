# Read Google’s 2010 guide for principles, then check the current instructions

**Six dated retirements in the 2010 guide’s advice require updated instructions, not a new ranking promise (Google operator announcements, 2012 to 2019).**

The guide’s advice to make useful material easier to find has aged better than its product instructions. We verified **six dated retirements** affecting services or features named in the supplied **2010** edition; several other old labels require separate treatment rather than an invented shutdown date. [Google, 2010, pp. 6, 14, 24 to 27 and 30; primary operator announcements in the table below].

An old manual can contain both a useful principle and an instruction that no longer works. You do not have to accept or reject the whole document at once. Check the sentence’s purpose, then check whether the named service, feature or policy still exists in the form described.

## What did Google promise in the original guide?

The introduction says the guide does not reveal secrets that automatically place a site first. Its purpose is to help search systems crawl, index and understand content while helping people use the site. That is operator guidance, not an experimental estimate of the benefit of each suggested action. [Google, 2010, p. 2].

The later content section suggests that publishers could “write an original piece of research.” It places that suggestion among ways to provide distinctive value for readers. There is no accompanying percentage improvement in rank or citation probability. [Google, 2010, p. 14].

We grade those historical recommendations B as primary statements of what Google advised. They are not A-grade causal evidence. A company can choose to publish useful research without claiming that the guide establishes the size of a resulting visibility gain.

The same care applies to the document’s examples. Its fictional baseball-card site helps explain page titles and navigation. It is an instructional example, not a reported customer outcome that can be transferred to a software company.

## Which dated retirements can we substantiate?

| Item or feature in the 2010 guide | Guide location | Verified retirement year | What the primary announcement establishes |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Google Website Optimizer, the standalone service | pp. 30 to 31 | 2012 | Google gave 1 August 2012 as the last day to access the standalone service and its experiment reports. The announcement introduced Analytics Content Experiments. |
| AdWords Keyword Tool | p. 14 and its link on p. 15 | 2013 | Google’s May 2013 Keyword Planner announcement said the Keyword Tool and Traffic Estimator would be sunset in about 60 days. We do not manufacture a precise final day from that notice. |
| Feature-phone Googlebot and related feature-phone sitemap/crawl tools | pp. 24 to 27 | 2016 | Google retired feature-phone crawling and associated tools. The announcement explicitly distinguishes smartphones. |
| DMOZ/Open Directory Project snippet fallback and NOODP control | pp. 6 to 7 | 2017 | Google stopped using DMOZ listings for snippets after DMOZ ceased operating; NOODP no longer had an effect. |
| HTML suggestions/content-analysis feature in old Webmaster Tools | pp. 6 and 30 | 2019 | Google’s migration announcement scheduled the old feature’s removal; it did not say clear titles and descriptions had become pointless. |
| Preferred-domain setting in Search Console | p. 11 link and p. 30 | 2019 | Google removed this account setting and no longer used its existing configuration. Canonicalization still had other signals. |

Primary announcements: [Website Optimizer](https://analytics.googleblog.com/2012/06/helping-to-create-better-websites.html), [Keyword Tool](https://adwords.googleblog.com/2013/05/introducing-keyword-planner-combining.html), [feature-phone tools](https://developers.google.com/search/blog/2016/11/an-update-on-googles-feature-phone), [DMOZ fallback](https://developers.google.com/search/blog/2017/06/better-snippets-for-your-users), [HTML suggestions](https://developers.google.com/search/blog/2019/01/focusing-on-new-search-console), and [preferred domain](https://developers.google.com/search/blog/2019/06/bye-bye-preferred-domain-setting). These are dated product/policy events, not estimated shares, so Wilson intervals do not apply.

The wording in the final column is deliberate. A replacement announced for a future period is not proof of a particular unreported last operating day. A feature being removed does not mean the problem it helped inspect has disappeared.

## Which old names need a different label?

Google Webmaster Tools was **renamed Search Console in 2015**. Calling that a product retirement would conceal continuity in the service. The present interface and available reports still need checking, but the old name alone is not evidence that the entire service ended. [Fink, 20 May 2015](https://developers.google.com/search/blog/2015/05/announcing-google-search-console-new).

The guide links to a Sitemap Generator hosted on Google Code. Google’s **2016** closure of that hosting service does not establish the retirement year of every individual script it hosted. We can substantiate the hosting event and identify the historical link. We cannot turn that into a verified claim about when the generator itself stopped being usable. [Google, 2010, p. 11; DiBona, 2015, Google Code closure notice](https://opensource.googleblog.com/2015/03/farewell-to-google-code.html).

Yahoo Site Explorer, the old robots.txt generator and the sitelink-removal control also appear in the guide’s historical instructions or linked features. We have not recovered a usable primary retirement-date record for each in this review. Their retirement years are withheld here. A repeated date in later marketing articles does not meet our standard for a verified operator date. [Google, 2010, pp. 21 and 30].

That is a remaining source gap, not permission to reuse the old directions. The safe starting point for an operational change is the operator’s current documentation. Brand names such as Google Analytics and the old Google Places label also need a product/version check; an old screenshot does not identify today’s interface or establish which historical version is being discussed.

## Which principles still have current operator support?

The current SEO Starter Guide continues to discuss descriptive URLs, helpful page titles, intelligible site organization and content that serves readers. It also continues to distinguish advice from guaranteed inclusion or position. We use the present guide for present Google guidance and the old PDF for the historical comparison. [Current SEO Starter Guide, sections Organize your site and Make your site interesting and useful].

The original guide’s discussion of clear navigation and descriptive anchors remains a useful way to think about a buyer’s task. If a reader cannot find a product’s scope or follow a reference to the supporting detail, adding an optimization label does not solve that communication problem. [Google, 2010, pp. 10 to 12 and 16 to 17].

The practical test can stay concrete. For fictional Cedarline Software, a product guide should identify which product it describes, let a reader reach the relevant instructions and keep obsolete instructions distinguishable from the current version. These are reader needs before they are hypotheses about search selection.

There is a trade-off when changing old URLs. A simpler address may be easier to understand, but a careless migration can break existing references. The old guide itself warns that URL rewriting can cause crawling problems when done incorrectly. Cosmetic tidiness is not a reason to discard working links without a migration plan. [Google, 2010, pp. 8 to 9].

## Which details require a modern reading?

The feature-phone section belongs to its period. Google’s present documentation describes smartphone crawling and mobile-first indexing, including access to equivalent primary content and resources. It recommends responsive design as a manageable configuration. Recreating a feature-phone sitemap merely because the old PDF mentions one would miss the documented setting. [Current mobile-first indexing guide, opening and Create a mobile-friendly site].

The link-qualification vocabulary has also developed. Current documentation distinguishes `sponsored`, `ugc` and `nofollow` relationships, and warns that a destination can still be discovered through other routes. The old comment-spam example is historical guidance, not a complete description of present link controls. [Google, 2010, pp. 22 to 23; current Qualify your outbound links guide].

Robots controls require their own boundary. Current Google documentation says robots.txt manages crawling and does not securely hide a resource or guarantee that its URL stays out of search. Authentication and indexing controls address different needs. Confusing those purposes can expose information or prevent a system from reading an intended indexing instruction. [Current Introduction to robots.txt, limitations section].

## What changes when the result is an AI answer?

Google currently states that the foundational SEO requirements still apply to its AI Overviews and AI Mode. A supporting page needs to be indexed and eligible for a Search snippet; meeting the requirements does not guarantee that Google will crawl, index or display it. This is guidance about Google’s features, not every answer engine. [AI features and your website, How to appear in AI features].

The documentation does not require an additional AI text file or a special schema type for those features. That matters when a vendor tries to turn an optional publishing convention into a universal admission ticket. A documented eligibility condition and a proposed tactic need different labels. [Same guide, SEO best practices].

The older guide cannot settle whether a particular B2B vendor will be named, whether a page will be cited or whether a buyer will visit it. Those observations require their own dated receipts. Keep the principle, replace the retired instruction, and leave the unmeasured effect open.

## What our own data shows

The September 2026 release has **2,095 valid target answers** from **2,100 planned target observations**, with five failures retained as exclusions. Across those valid targets, **39 have an empty supplied citation list**, **1.86%**, descriptive **95% Wilson interval 1.36% to 2.53%**. [Absence Index release 2026-09](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.22907695); [answer-citation bases](../../data/answer-citation-bases.csv).

Those outcomes do not test whether following Google’s guide caused a citation. We have no controlled measurement of that effect on this point. The instruction and the observed answer remain separate records, each with its own date and purpose.
