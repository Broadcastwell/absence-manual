# Search was already an answer-seeking habit in the 2009 SEO Bible

**The book prescribes both 5% to 7% and 7% to 10% keyword density, without a supporting experiment (Ledford, 2009, pp. 67, 99).**

The same book describes **5% to 7%** keyword density as a strict Google range, then offers **7% to 10%** as a general guideline. Neither passage supplies an experiment establishing the prescription. [Ledford, 2009, printed pp. 67 and 99, PDF pp. 89 and 121].

Its interviews have aged differently. Jessica Bowman describes people seeking answers; Rand Fishkin discusses instant answers; Matt Naeger discusses Wikipedia’s place in brand searches; Jill Whalen questions a stable ranking number. Those are useful historical voices, provided we resist turning them into empirical predictions about systems that the book did not study.

## What kind of source is the book?

Jerri L. Ledford’s second edition is a practitioner guide published in **2009**. It combines explanations, recommendations, worksheets and interviews with people working in search marketing. It is not a single empirical study with a defined sample, common outcome and statistical analysis. [Book title and publication pages; Appendix B].

That mixed format needs more than one evidence label. A dated interview can establish that a named practitioner expressed a view. A technical recommendation needs a current operator check. An unattributed performance statistic needs its original dataset and method before it can become a quantitative claim.

We grade attributed testimony B for the historical statement and unsupported effect claims C. The grade follows the use. It would be unfair to reject an interview because it is not an experiment, and equally mistaken to treat the interview as though it were one.

The book’s age also creates a reading problem. Names of services and interfaces can make a passage feel obsolete even when its question remains useful. Elsewhere, a familiar word such as keyword can make an unsupported prescription seem current. Familiarity works in both directions.

## What did Bowman say about answers?

Bowman says people use search engines like a “question-and-answer machine.” Her surrounding discussion concerns company, product and service information, favorable and unfavorable sources, and the way public-relations teams might respond. [Jessica Bowman interview, printed p. **397**, PDF p. **419**].

This is an observation about what people want from search and how organizations encounter public information about themselves. It is a recognizable ancestor of current questions about answers. It is not a description of how a later generative system chooses sources, trains on information or decides which vendor to name.

The context also gives the statement a limit. Bowman is discussing a search results page containing a variety of sources, including video. She is not reporting a controlled comparison of answer formats, citation rates or buyer behavior. Quoting the phrase should preserve its date and setting.

If you manage a software product, the user need still makes sense: someone may want an answer about compatibility, suitability or a limitation rather than a generic brand page. That practical interpretation is ours. The interview does not quantify the visibility benefit of rewriting a page around that question.

## What did Fishkin include among search experiences?

Fishkin lists “Instant answers (like Ask.com)” among the areas of vertical search. He places them beside local, product, image, news and travel search. [Rand Fishkin interview, printed p. **416**, PDF p. **438**].

The list is useful because it prevents a false history in which search previously meant only an undifferentiated list of links. Practitioners were already distinguishing different ways to retrieve and present information. Their categories need not map neatly onto the products or terminology used today.

The statement does not demonstrate that any particular modern answer engine is a direct implementation of his prediction. Nor does describing a growing area identify a ranking or citation mechanism. Historical resemblance and a tested explanation are separate claims.

Fishkin also offers opinions about other search approaches in the same interview. We do not treat every forecast as a verified fact simply because one phrase now sounds familiar. A source review should preserve the selective nature of a retrospective comparison rather than finding only the lines that seem prescient.

## What did Naeger say about Wikipedia?

Naeger says “Wikipedia consistently ranks well” while discussing Google searches for brand information. He frames the problem as understanding which pages appear for a brand and what those pages say about it. [Matt Naeger interview, printed p. **437**, PDF p. **459**].

This is practitioner testimony about a visible pattern. It is not the source of the later Wikipedia-position percentages quoted elsewhere in this research section. Those belong to Lewandowski and colleagues’ separate empirical sample and must retain that sample’s denominator.

The distinction is more than a citation formality. An interview observation can motivate a question. A later measured distribution can address part of it. The distribution still does not establish that an organization can control an independent encyclopedia or that editing an encyclopedia entry will cause an AI answer to change.

Naeger himself resists the idea of ultimate control in the surrounding discussion. That makes the interview less convenient as a sales promise and more useful as a record of a recurring problem: organizations want to influence public information while the information is selected and described by others. [Same interview page].

## Why did Whalen question rank tracking?

Whalen calls rankings “an impossible measurement” in a discussion of personalization, geographic targeting and results that differ between users. She proposes looking at search traffic, conversions and sales when assessing success. [Jill Whalen interview, printed p. **448**, PDF p. **470**].

The quotation should not be stretched into a claim that no search result can ever be recorded. A dated result under stated conditions is observable. The problem is treating one recorded position as a stable, universal description of what everyone sees or as a complete business outcome.

There is an interesting tension within the book. Its later rank-tracking worksheet provides a baseline and monthly columns for recording rank. The worksheet can be useful as a record, but it does not resolve Whalen’s objection about what that record represents. [Appendix D, printed p. **481**, PDF p. **503**].

A similar tension appears in AI visibility reporting. A company may want a simple recurring number, while the observation depends on the question, engine, repetition and valid-answer base. The desire for a compact report does not remove those conditions. It changes how carefully the report must define its number.

## Where does the keyword-density advice conflict?

| Original location | What the book asserts | What we can accept |
|---|---|---|
| Printed p. 67, PDF p. 89 | Google is strict about a 5% to 7% range; other engines allow about 5%, with exclusion warnings | A historical prescription in the text; no supporting trial or operator specification is supplied there |
| Printed p. 94, PDF p. 116 | Experts disagree about the right density, and no firm rule is established | An explicit admission of uncertainty within the same book |
| Printed p. 99, PDF p. 121 | A general 7% to 10% guideline, with an example distributing keywords across text and metadata | Another historical prescription whose scope and wording differ from the earlier strict range |

These percentages are proposed targets, not measured success shares. They therefore have no recoverable binomial base or Wilson interval. The book does not report an intervention establishing a visibility gain from following either range.

There is also a definition issue. The example on p. 94 describes density relative to page words, while the p. 99 example tells a reader to distribute its keywords between text and metadata. Before comparing values, you would need a consistent rule about which text and repeated phrases enter the calculation. The book does not resolve that boundary sufficiently to make its prescriptions a reproducible ranking formula.

We do not average the bands, choose the one that looks easier to satisfy or call either a current Google requirement. The internally conflicting advice is useful as an evidence-reading example. A confident numerical range needs a source for the rule as well as a clear definition of the number.

## What should a B2B reader keep from the book?

The interviews identify questions that remain recognizable: what people want to know, which outside sources describe the organization, how search formats differ and what a measurement actually represents. Read those as dated perspectives from named practitioners, not as proof of a present optimization effect.

For fictional Cedarline Software, that might prompt a specific editorial question about its integration guide. Does a reader learn which version is supported and where the limitation comes from? The answer can justify a clearer explanation without claiming that a historic density percentage or an interview forecast dictates the engine’s response.

The old book does not tell us how much that revision would change citation. Nor does its rank worksheet tell us whether a compact monthly score has hidden a change in question mix. The unresolved measurement problem is more useful to retain than an apparently timeless numerical recipe.

## What our own data shows

Across September 2026, **1,259 of 14,192 complete vendor-question-engine groups** have mixed naming across three valid repeats: **8.9%**, descriptive **95% Wilson interval 8.4% to 9.4%**. Mixed means the vendor is named in one or two repeats rather than none or all three. [Absence Index release 2026-09](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.22907695); [complete-repeat table](../../data/repeat-disagreement.csv).

The unit is a checked vendor within a question and engine, not a unique company or a population of buyers. Groups are dependent through shared questions and answers, so the interval is descriptive. This does not reproduce a 2009 Google ranking study or establish that Whalen predicted a numerical AI-answer result. It records a present measurement boundary beside her historical warning.
